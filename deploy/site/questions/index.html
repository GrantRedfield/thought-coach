The four questions

Grandmaster Jacob Aagaard teaches three questions to ask in every position; Thought Coach adds a fourth about your candidate moves. The order below is the order strong players actually work in: we reviewed 350 rapid and classical games by strong players, counting what they drew and said, and put the questions in their order. Ask them while you play, answer them on the board or out loud, and the review shows you which ones you skip and what skipping them cost.

  1. 1

    What does your opponent want?

    After every move they play, ask what they would do if it were their move again: a threat, a plan, a piece heading somewhere. Masters start here: most of their long thinks begin with the opponent's position, not their own moves. It is also the question club players skip most, and missing it is the most expensive mistake.

    Answer it by
    Drawing an arrow from their piece for the move they want to play.
    The review checks
    Stockfish's best move for them if they could move again. Real threats you drew count as seen.
    The oversight it catches
    Walking into a tactic, or a move that was safe only by luck.
  2. 2

    Where are the weaknesses?

    Weak pawns (isolated, doubled, backward), holes that no pawn can cover again, and pieces left undefended. Look at both sides: the weaknesses you can attack, and the ones your own moves leave behind. Of the questions strong players answer out loud, this is the one they answer most consistently.

    Answer it by
    Circling the square (right-click it), or saying it: "d5 is a hole", "his e-pawn is weak".
    The review checks
    The weaknesses Stockfish's best line actually goes after, and whether you pointed at them first.
    The oversight it catches
    A pawn move that gives away a square for good, or a piece left hanging.
  3. 3

    What are your two or three candidates?

    Before you play, list your options: captures, checks and threats first, then quieter moves. Masters usually weigh two or three, rarely more. Even so, the engine's best move is among the candidates they record only about 59% of the time (our master data; what they record is not everything they think). The aim isn't to find everything, just to give the right move a fair chance to make the shortlist, and to trust it once it has.

    Answer it by
    Drawing an arrow from your piece for each move you consider.
    The review checks
    Whether Stockfish's best move was on your list, or on it and then turned down.
    The oversight it catches
    Never even considering the right move, or seeing it and talking yourself out of it.
  4. 4

    Which piece is doing least?

    Usually one piece does nothing: a bishop behind its own pawns, a knight on the rim, a rook that hasn't got into the game yet. Masters rarely say it out loud: they answer it by making one of their candidates a move that brings that piece into play.

    Answer it by
    Drawing an arrow for a move that brings it back to life, or saying it ("my bishop is terrible").
    The review checks
    Your least active piece by the squares it can reach, and the move Stockfish uses to improve it.
    The oversight it catches
    Playing on with a piece out of the game while the opponent's pieces get better.

A routine for each move

  1. They moved: what do they want? Draw their threat.
  2. Scan the board. Circle the weaknesses, both sides.
  3. Two or three candidates. An arrow for each, one of them for your least active piece if it has a good square. Then pick.
  4. One last look at what your move allows before you let go of the piece.

When to think hard

Masters don't spend their time evenly. They play the moves they know quickly and save their long thinks for the moments the position changes character: leaving the opening, a pawn break, an exchange that changes the structure, a threat appearing. Do the same: the full routine at those moments, a quick check of question 1 on the rest. In time trouble, just play: moves played with under 30 seconds left aren't judged on your thinking.
