The four questions

Grandmaster Jacob Aagaard teaches three questions to ask in every position. Thought Coach adds a fourth about your candidate moves. Ask them while you play, answer them on the board or out loud, and the review shows you which ones you skip and what skipping them cost.

  1. Q1

    Where are the weaknesses?

    Weak pawns (isolated, doubled, backward), holes that no pawn can cover again, and pieces left undefended. Look at both sides: the weaknesses you can attack, and the ones your own moves leave behind.

    Answer it by
    Circling the square (right-click it), or saying it: "d5 is a hole", "his e-pawn is weak".
    The review checks
    The weaknesses Stockfish's best line actually goes after, and whether you pointed at them first.
    The oversight it catches
    A pawn move that gives away a square for good, or a piece left hanging.
  2. Q2

    Which is your worst piece?

    Usually one piece does nothing: a bishop behind its own pawns, a knight on the rim, a rook that hasn't got into the game yet. Improving it is often the best plan when nothing tactical is going on.

    Answer it by
    Saying it ("my bishop is terrible"), or drawing an arrow for a move that brings it back to life.
    The review checks
    Your least active piece by the squares it can reach, and the move Stockfish uses to improve it.
    The oversight it catches
    Playing on with a piece out of the game while the opponent's pieces get better.
  3. Q3

    What does your opponent want?

    After every move they play, ask what they would do if it were their move again. A threat, a plan, a piece heading somewhere. This is the question most often skipped, and missing it is the most expensive mistake.

    Answer it by
    Drawing an arrow from their piece for the move they want to play.
    The review checks
    Stockfish's best move for them if they could move again. Real threats you drew count as seen.
    The oversight it catches
    Walking into a tactic, or a move that was safe only by luck.
  4. Q4

    Is the best move among your candidates?

    Before you play, list your options. Captures, checks and threats first, then quieter moves. The aim isn't to find everything, just to make sure the right move made the shortlist.

    Answer it by
    Drawing an arrow from your piece for each move you consider.
    The review checks
    Whether Stockfish's best move was on your list, or on it and then turned down.
    The oversight it catches
    Never even considering the right move, or seeing it and talking yourself out of it.

A routine for each move

  1. They moved: what do they want? Draw their threat (Q3).
  2. Scan the board. Circle the weaknesses (Q1) and name your worst piece (Q2).
  3. List your candidates. An arrow for each one (Q4), then pick.
  4. One last look at what your move allows before you let go of the piece.

You don't have to do all four every move. Use them when the position is new to you or when something has just changed. In time trouble, just play: moves played with under 30 seconds left aren't judged on your thinking.
