How strong players think: data and methods

Thought Coach compares your thinking habits with strong players' on the same measures. This page says where those benchmarks come from, how they are measured, what they show, and where they fall short. Last updated 23 November 2026.

1. The dataset

Some strong players stream or record their games while drawing arrows on the board and talking through their thinking. Those recordings are the closest thing available to a strong player's thought process during real play. Thought Coach read the board, the arrows and the speech from them and put each game through the same review your games get.

  • Players: six strong players, five of them grandmasters or international masters. Each player needs at least 3 reviewed games, and counts equally however many games they have (28 to 92 each), so no one prolific player stands for "masters". Benchmarks are only ever shown pooled: no names, games or quotes.
  • Games: 350 rapid and classical games (lichess's rapid line and slower). 192 blitz and bullet games were processed but are left out: at that speed players draw and say much less (for one player, arrows fell from 2.3 per move in rapid to 0.5 in 3-minute games and 0.1 in bullet), so the measures would mostly measure silence.
  • Ratings: in the 270 games with ratings in the game record, the players' online rating had a median of 2523 (10th to 90th percentile: 1948 to 2689; the low end is fresh accounts climbing from scratch) and their opponents' a median of 1963 (1435 to 2315). Strong players often face much weaker opponents in these videos: see the limits.
  • Selection: players who regularly draw arrows and think out loud in rapid or classical games, with videos where the board, arrows and speech can be read reliably. Speed-chess series, gimmick games, odds games and games that couldn't be matched to a game record are left out.
  • Moves: 8,465 of the players' own moves were reviewed; 6,205 of them (73%) had recorded thinking behind them (an arrow, a circle or a spoken move).
  • Titled Thought Coach players: players with a lichess title who use Thought Coach are added to the benchmarks automatically, each as one more player, unless they switch it off (see privacy). None are included yet.
  • Sources: publicly posted videos. The videos, transcripts and quotes are not published here. The exceptions, the sample review and the example card on the home page, are shared with that player's permission.

2. How thinking is measured

What counts as "in your thinking"

A move is in your recorded thinking if you drew an arrow for it or said it out loud before playing your move. Arrows for your own moves are candidates; arrows for the opponent's moves are their ideas. Speech is turned into text and chess moves are picked out of it. Anything you thought but didn't draw or say isn't in the record, and the review never claims you "didn't think of" something: only that it isn't in what was recorded.

The engine

Every position is analysed with Stockfish. A move's cost is the win chance it gives up (lichess's win-chance curve). A move that costs 5% or more is costly. The opponent's threat is what they would play if it were their move again; it is real if allowing it costs 10% or more.

The measures

MeasureWhat is counted
Real threats left unstoppedReal threats your move did not stop, whether or not you drew them (lower is better)
Quiet threats named earlyThreats that aren't captures (a fork being set up, a mating idea) that you drew or said before they could happen
Weaknesses pointed atWeak pawns, holes and loose pieces the engine's best line used, that you marked or targeted
Best move among your candidatesPositions where you drew something, and the engine's best move was among it
Least active piece brought into playClearly passive pieces you named, or improved with a candidate
Good moves drawn, then droppedPositions where you drew the best move but played a costly one instead (lower is better)
Critical positions that went wrongPositions where only one or two moves hold, where your move was costly (lower is better)
Moves in time troubleMoves played with under 30 seconds on the clock (lower is better)

Moves made in time trouble aren't held against your thinking: there is no time to think out loud there.

What the review can and can't know about a move

For each idea that mattered (the engine's best move, the opponent's threat, a weakness, a passive piece), the review puts what it knows into one of five states, and its wording follows the state:

StateWhenHow the review says it
RecordedYou drew it or said it before your move"you drew Nf5", "you'd seen their Qh4"
Considered, then rejectedYou drew it but played something else"you drew it and then played…"
No recorded evidenceYou drew or said other moves, but not this one"not in your recorded thinking"
Likely oversightNo recorded evidence, and your move walked into it (their threat came next, or the move you played lost to it)"your move didn't stop it"
Not enough to judgeNothing was drawn or said on that move, or you were in time troublenot judged as thinking at all

Not drawing or saying an idea doesn't mean you didn't think of it. That is why every story card has a Feedback on this card button with "I did consider this, I just didn't draw or say it": those answers are stored as claims to check against the evidence, not as facts, and used to measure how often the review's reading is wrong.

How it is built

  • Arrows and circles are recorded by the browser extension with their timing, and matched to the move you were thinking about. Arrows from your pieces are candidates; arrows from theirs are their ideas.
  • Speech is transcribed by a speech-to-text model (Whisper, small English model) on our servers; chess moves and squares are picked out of the text by rules, and mood words (worried, upbeat, giving up) by word lists, with a small local language model reading the harder sentences where it is available.
  • The engine is Stockfish, at 250,000 nodes per position, with the top five lines kept as the engine's candidates. Win chances use lichess's published curve. The first opening moves played from the book aren't judged.
  • The coaching picks one focus per game from what cost the most win chance, nudged toward what keeps coming back across your games and toward where you're furthest from the benchmarks.

Validation so far

What is checked: on the master games, that the moves read from video match the real game record, that the arrows land on legal moves, and that move times line up with the game's clocks (games that fail are fixed or left out). What is not yet measured: how accurate the speech tagging and the mood reading are against human labels, and how often a player's "I considered it" corrects the review. Those numbers will be published once there is enough labelled feedback.

How much data a conclusion needs

  • A master needs 3 or more reviewed games to count, and a measure at least 5 cases per master.
  • You are compared with masters on a habit once you have 10 or more cases of it.
  • Whether your focus is working is judged only after 2 or more games, as a probability with a sceptical prior: one good game isn't called a habit.
  • Every rate on your home page shows its likely range, so a small sample looks uncertain.

4. Findings

Descriptive findings from this dataset. They describe these six players in these games; they are not established laws of chess thinking.

Few candidates, chosen well

The typical move has 2 or 3 candidates drawn; 8 or more on only about 1 move in 20. When the best move is among them, it is played most of the time: a drawn best move is dropped for a costly one only about 6% of the time.

Threats are mostly answered, not announced

Strong players name only a minority of threats aloud, even quiet ones (about 14%), yet leave only about 5% of real threats unstopped. Most threats (an attacked piece, a hanging pawn) are handled without a word. That is why Thought Coach measures threats left unstopped, not threats named.

Wider searchers went wrong less in critical positions

The players who drew about 3 candidates per move went wrong in critical positions about half as often (7–8%) as those who usually drew 2 (13–18%). With six players this is a hint, not a result, and part of it is time control: the narrower searchers played faster games more often.

More than one strong way to think

Some strong players spell out threats and weaknesses constantly; others talk mostly about plans and structure and still play at the same accuracy. The benchmarks are an average of styles, so a gap on one measure is a question to look at, not a verdict.

5. Limits

  • Six players. Averages over a handful of people are uncertain: the clearest gaps (good moves dropped, critical positions) are well beyond the players' range, but smaller differences can be noise. More players would roughly halve the uncertainty by about 20.
  • Thinking on camera isn't silent thinking. Players narrating for an audience may draw and say more, or differently, than they would alone. The same is true for you: thinking out loud can itself change how you play, usually by slowing you down.
  • Weaker opponents. The masters' opponents had a median rating near 1960. Some rates (threats left unstopped, critical positions) may look better than they would against equals.
  • Who streams. Players who think out loud on video are not a random sample of strong players; their styles may lean toward explaining, and toward openings and positions that make good content.
  • Only what's recorded. A move thought of but not drawn or said is invisible, for masters and for you.
  • Reading video isn't perfect. Moves, arrows and speech are read automatically; reviews are checked for timing and legality, and games that don't pass are left out, but small errors remain.
  • Not validated against improvement. The measures are associated with good play in these games; there is no controlled study yet showing that changing them raises a rating.
  • Not a rating-group comparison. There isn't yet enough data from club players to say how these habits differ by rating. That will come from players who use Thought Coach, pooled and anonymous.
