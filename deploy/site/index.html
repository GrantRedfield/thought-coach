What no engine review can see

Thought Coach records how you think: your arrows and your voice

An engine only sees the move you played. While you play on lichess, Thought Coach also captures every arrow you draw and every word you say, tied to the exact move. That's what makes the coaching about you.

Your arrows

Right-click arrows and circles, exactly as you already do on lichess. They show the moves you weighed, the threats you spotted, and the squares you worried about.

You drew Nf5 and Qh5, then played Qh5. Was Nf5 the move you dropped?

Your voice

Think out loud as you play. Your words become text, matched to the move you were on: your plans, your calculations, your doubts, the moment you decided it was lost.

"I don't think he can take on e5." He could, and the review shows why.

Coaching on your thinking

Checked against Stockfish, move by move: was the best move ever in your thinking? Did you see their threat and let it through? Did your confidence match the position?

One habit to work on next game, with the moment from this game that shows why.

Voice is optional and easy to switch off; arrows alone still give a full review. The more you say, the more precisely the coaching can pinpoint what to fix.

Four questions strong players ask

The first three are Grandmaster Jacob Aagaard's; the fourth is about your candidate moves. They're not homework: each one catches a different kind of mistake, and the review shows which ones your recorded thinking skips.

1

What does my opponent want?

Most collapses start with an idea of theirs that was never answered.

Draw their best move as an arrow before you choose yours.

2

Where are the weaknesses?

Plans come from targets: a loose pawn, a hole, an exposed king, on both sides.

Before a pawn move, circle the squares it stops guarding.

3

Which piece is doing least?

Improving your worst piece is often the best quiet move on the board.

Make one of your candidates a move for that piece.

4

What are my candidates?

The best move can't be chosen if it's never on the list, or is dropped too soon.

Draw two different moves before you play one.

How to use them in a game

The mental game

The habits behind the mistakes

Many losses aren't about chess knowledge at all. When you think out loud, the review can see the moments your mood, confidence or clock took over, and it names a habit for each one.

Sure, but wrong

Confidence the position didn't back

You sounded certain on the moves that went wrong, while the engine had it level or worse.

Habit: when a move feels obvious, check what it allows.

Giving up early

Calling it lost too soon

You said it was over while the engine still gave you real chances, and the moves after that got looser.

Habit: find the most stubborn move and let the board decide.

The inner critic

Judging instead of playing

After a mistake the talk turned on yourself, and the next few moves cost more than the mistake did.

Habit: say what happened in one plain sentence, then back to the board.

One move too late

Seeing it just after moving

The right idea came out of your mouth seconds after you let go of the piece.

Habit: look once more before you release it.

Time in the wrong place

Fast where it mattered

Long thinks on quiet moves, quick ones where only one move held. Every move's time is matched with how critical it was.

Habit: after a capture, check or surprise, slow down.

Comfortable, then caught

Relaxing when winning

Upbeat talk, a winning position, then a fast move that gave much of it back.

Habit: when it's going well, keep asking about their checks and threats.

These are read from what you draw and say and how long you take, checked against the engine: never guesses about your personality. Mood needs your voice; the clock and the arrows work without it. Each one shows up only when your own games show it. Good decisions get named too: the only move found under pressure, a threat called before it came, a candidate list that held the best move.

Improvement you can measure

Play, review, practise, measure

A coach doesn't hand you twenty notes after every game. Thought Coach gives you one focus, practice positions from your own games, and then checks your next games to see whether the habit is actually changing.

  1. Play

    A rapid or classical game on lichess, drawing your ideas and, if you like, thinking out loud.

  2. Review

    The story of the game and one focus for the next one, with the reason from this game.

  3. Practise

    Positions from your own games where your thinking slipped: find their threat, find three candidates, trust the move you'd already found.

  4. Measure

    Your next games' rates for that habit, with how sure the change is, so one lucky game isn't called progress.

Three steps to your first review

About two minutes to set up, then play the way you normally do.

  1. Connect lichess and add the extension

    Sign in with lichess (we never see your password) and add the free Chrome extension.

  2. Play, and capture your thinking

    Play a rapid or classical game. Right-click arrows for the moves you consider and their threats; think out loud if you like.

  3. Review your decisions

    A few minutes after the game, your story and full review are ready: what your thinking got right, and the one habit to work on.

Methods, data and limits

Fair play, privacy, open source

Built for after the game
  • No move suggestions, evaluations or hints during a game.
  • Nothing is shown while you play.
  • Analysis starts only after lichess says the game is over.

Not affiliated with or endorsed by lichess.

Your data stays yours
  • Voice is optional, and the audio is deleted once it's turned into text.
  • Not sold, not used for ads or to train AI models.
  • Delete everything from the setup page, instantly.
